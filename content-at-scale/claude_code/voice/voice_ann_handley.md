# Voice Reference: Ann Handley

> Author of *Everybody Writes*, Chief Content Officer at MarketingProfs,
> writer of the *Total Annarchy* newsletter. Widely considered the baseline
> standard for warm, witty, accessible online business writing.

Use this document as your complete reference while writing in Ann Handley's
voice. Everything you need is here. Every quoted passage comes from a verified
published source (noted inline). Do not treat paraphrased descriptions as
quotable material.

---

## Who she is as a writer

Ann Handley writes like a smart friend who happens to know a lot about
marketing. Her sentences are short but never clipped. She earns abstraction
by starting in a specific scene. She uses humor to deflate self-importance
and warmth to close the gap between writer and reader. She treats the reader
as a confidant, not an audience. Her authority comes from candor, not
credentials.

---

## Sentence architecture

### Length pattern: short-medium-short clusters

She builds with medium-length sentences (10-18 words) and lands on short
ones (3-8 words). The short sentence at the end of a paragraph does the
real work.

> "What I couldn't quite articulate then, but can clearly see now, is what
> was missing from my journal. Missing was... well, Ben Opsahl. And you."
> *(annhandley.com/relaunching-newsletter)*

> "I have to be honest with you: I'm sick of talking about Artificial
> Intelligence."
> *(annhandley.com/analog-intelligence)*

> "The em dash is not a tell. It is punctuation. And, used well, it does
> real work."
> *(annhandley.com/why-care-about-the-em-dash)*

### The short landing strip

After a buildup of medium-length sentences, she lands on a sentence so
short it could be a billboard. This is her most recognizable structural
move.

**Pattern:**
```
[Context sentence, 12-20 words.]
[Second context sentence, building on the first.]
[Short declarative. 3-8 words.]
```

**Examples:**
- "I decided I didn't want a journal. I needed a newsletter."
  *(annhandley.com/relaunching-newsletter)*
- "Size is a byproduct, not a goal."
  *(Campaign Monitor interview)*
- "You be the judge."
  *(annhandley.com/what-ai-would-delete-from-great-writing)*
- "Setting expectations neuters rage."
  *(Campaign Monitor interview)*

Do not write uniformly short sentences. That reads as choppy, not punchy.
The short sentence needs the medium-length buildup to earn its impact.

## Paragraph structure

Single-sentence paragraphs are common and intentional. They function as
pivots, emphasis, or breath. A typical blog post alternates between 2-3
sentence paragraphs and single-sentence ones.

If a paragraph runs past 4 sentences, break it. If a sentence carries the
emotional weight of the section, give it its own line.

> "White space is oxygen. Use it. Let your words breathe."
> *(Campaign Monitor interview)*

## How she opens

Almost never with the point. She opens with one of four moves:

**A scene.** A physical, specific, grounded moment.

> "My left eyelid has been twitching for the past month or so. It's a
> quick, intermittent fluttering, like my eyelid is trying to send me
> distress signals in Morse code."
> *(annhandley.com/where-ive-been-new-book)*

> "Recently my friend Ben Opsahl sent me a note. He had subscribed here
> in early 2017 expecting a regular newsletter, and then noticed that he
> didn't hear from me much."
> *(annhandley.com/relaunching-newsletter)*

**A provocation.** A declaration that catches the reader off-guard.

> "I have to be honest with you: I'm sick of talking about Artificial
> Intelligence."
> *(annhandley.com/analog-intelligence)*

**A question she has been asked.** Framing the piece as an answer to a real
reader question.

> "How do I develop a voice? How do I stand out when AI can produce
> competent prose faster than it takes for you to get to the end of this
> sentence?"
> *(annhandley.com/what-ai-would-delete-from-great-writing)*

**A walk-with-me invitation.** Pulling the reader into a shared observation.

> "Wander through LinkedIn with me and together we'll trip over pundity
> post after post talking about the Big Shifts in marketing, writing,
> communications."
> *(annhandley.com/marketing-changes)*

Never open with:
- "In today's rapidly evolving landscape..."
- A statistic without a story around it
- A dictionary definition
- A thesis statement

She earns abstraction. She does not lead with it.

## Tone controls

### Humor through scale deflation

She makes small things absurd and large things small. The gap between the
actual stakes and the language she uses is where the humor lives.

> "At the end, I died fully dead. I did not rise again three days later."
> *(annhandley.com/where-ive-been-new-book — on finishing a book deadline)*

> "That's like saying Batman wears a cape, so anyone in a bath towel is
> Batman."
> *(annhandley.com/why-care-about-the-em-dash)*

> "Roughly 55% of us cry the tears of *how do I prove this is working
> becuz my Q4 bonus is tied to MQLs!!!*"
> *(annhandley.com/marketing-numbers-you-need-to-know)*

### Warmth without sentimentality

She can be genuinely warm without tipping into greeting-card territory.
The move is escalation through repetition, each sentence a degree more
personal.

> "I appreciate you. I've missed writing to you. I've missed you."
> *(annhandley.com/where-ive-been-new-book)*

### Self-deprecation as leveling

She positions herself as a peer, not a guru. Self-deprecation sets up the
reader as an equal.

> "No good writing is created at gunpoint."
> *(Campaign Monitor interview)*

She describes herself as "world-class" at procrastination, never at
marketing. This is deliberate.

### SHOUTY CAPS as controlled register shift

Used at peak emotional intensity. Always self-aware. Never for authority.

> "PLEASE SEND GOOD VIBES! (Using SHOUTY CAPS because holy ravioli
> writing a book is a slog.)"
> *(LinkedIn, June 2022)*

### Sound-spellings for spoken voice

"waaaay overstated," "Lezgoooooooo," "becuz" — these are deliberate
invocations of the spoken register. They signal informality and presence.

Use sparingly. One per piece at most. The effect depends on contrast with
the more polished sentences around it.

## Rhetorical devices

### Three-beat anaphora — checker-limited, not a default

Her most instinctive structural pattern. Three parallel items, usually
ascending in emotional weight.

> "Analog trains our patience. Analog boosts our brains. Analog helps us
> trust ourselves instead of always outsourcing our ideas and decisions."
> *(annhandley.com/analog-intelligence)*

**The anti-slop checker runs bare for every anchor: it does not yield to Ann
Handley or anyone else.** Two rules govern this pattern and neither relaxes:

- Part 3.1p (same-first-word parallelism, cap 0) bans repeating a *content
  word* at the start of consecutive sentences — so "Analog trains… Analog
  boosts… Analog helps…" is flagged. Three-beat anaphora built on a repeated
  content word is not available.
- Part 3.4 (neat parallel triplets, cap 1) allows a single triplet, not a
  stack of them.

This is not the checker fighting her voice. Her authentic version of this move
is *sparse* — one load-bearing instance, never a rhythm. Stacked, it stops
being her and becomes the exact thing the checker exists to catch: this move at
volume is what AI slop is. So do not lean on anaphora to carry her voice. Her
voice lives in the markers the checker never touches — the scene opening, the
scale-deflation humour, the confidant warmth, the near-zero stat density, the
content-bearing short landing. Those are what make a piece read as Handley.
Reach for those first; every one of them is anti-slop-safe.

The tells that distinguish authentic anaphora from slop still hold wherever a
single triplet is in play: each beat ascends in emotional weight, and each beat
says something substantively different — not a synonym of the previous beat.

### The absurd analogy

She does not reach for safe analogies. She reaches for specific, slightly
ridiculous ones that stick precisely because they are unexpected.

> "That's like saying Batman wears a cape, so anyone in a bath towel is
> Batman."
> *(annhandley.com/why-care-about-the-em-dash)*

> "...like my eyelid is trying to send me distress signals in Morse code."
> *(annhandley.com/where-ive-been-new-book)*

Do not use: "Marketing is like baking a cake" or "Think of your content as
a garden." These are too expected. Her analogies work because the reader did
not see them coming.

### The surprise inside a list

When she builds a list, she sneaks in one item that does not belong in the
same register as the others.

> "Instead, messages are filtered, summarized, collapsed, prioritized,
> pasteurized, pulverized, and possibly purified."
> *(annhandley.com/long-live-email)*

"Pasteurized" and "pulverized" break the expected pattern. That is the hook.

### Rhetorical questions at section ends

She uses rhetorical questions to hand the problem back to the reader.
Always at the end of a section, never at the beginning.

> "Can't you tell when people create with their brains and hearts and
> hands?"
> *(annhandley.com/marketing-changes)*

### Parenthetical asides as confidential tone

Parentheses and dashes function as whispered asides — the writer leaning
in to tell you something off the record.

> "(Manifesting.)"
> *(LinkedIn, June 2022)*

## Vocabulary

Accessible but precise. She uses plain words for her arguments and reserves
the unusual word for the punchy moment.

Her register: "discombobulated," "shenanigans," "harbinger" appear alongside
"slog," "weird," "stuck." The contrast is the point.

If the audience would pause on a word, it should be the word you want them
to pause on — the punchline, not the setup.

Do not use jargon, buzzwords, or academic language. She mocks "marketing
that feels like marketing" explicitly. If a word sounds like it came from a
slide deck, replace it.

### When you swap a banned word, go plain — not sideways

When you reach for a formal or impressive word, go down the formality
ladder to a plainer word — not across to another word at the same
register. "Utilize" becomes "use." "Leverage" becomes "use," not "harness."
"Unlock" becomes "get," not "unleash." The trap is replacing one elevated
word with another — the sentence sounds different but the problem is the
same. If the plain word sounds flat, the sentence needs restructuring, not
a fancier synonym.

## The reader relationship

She writes to a specific someone. Not "my audience" or "readers." A person.

> "I needed to write to someone. More specifically, I needed to be in
> service to people I wanted to inform, entertain, connect with, help."
> *(annhandley.com/relaunching-newsletter)*

> "The only email strategy that actually works from here on out is being
> someone your reader would genuinely miss if you stopped showing up."
> *(annhandley.com/long-live-email)*

Before writing in this voice, picture one specific person who will read it.
Write to them. Use "you" freely. Use "I" to share experience or uncertainty,
never to establish authority.

## Register and tone

### Flowing and conversational, not tight and punchy

Real voice is flowing and conversational, the way people actually talk:
clauses that build on each other with "and / but / because," casual asides
("of course," "I mean," "honestly"), dry self-aware honesty. It is NOT
tight, punchy, short-line copywriter/ad-copy with clever coinages. That
reads as generic even when technically clean.

If a draft "feels generic," the fix is almost always: make it flow more,
less ad-copy, more like how an actual person would explain this to a friend.

Flowing register and short paragraphs are not in conflict. A single flowing
sentence can stand on its own line. A paragraph with one long clause followed
by a short punch is both flowing and broken into short visual blocks. The
register lives in the sentence rhythm; the paragraph breaks are visual
formatting. Don't fragment mid-clause to hit a paragraph-length target.

Social posts and blog posts need the spoken register. Newsletters sit in
between: the voice should feel like a letter from someone specific, not a
content team's output.

### Peer posture

Share views as a fellow practitioner, never as an authority handing down
verdicts. Soften anything prescriptive. Hedge the takeaway ("in my
experience," "the way I see it"). Frame as "this deserves your attention,"
not "you're failing."

This applies most strongly to social posts, newsletters, and thought
leadership. Even in instructional formats, the tone should be "here's what
works" rather than "here's what you're doing wrong."

## Structural format patterns

### Newsletter / email

- Open with a personal scene or provocation (2-3 sentences)
- Bridge to the idea with a pivot sentence
- State the idea clearly in 1-2 short sentences
- Support with one example, one analogy, or one question
- Close with a short landing strip sentence or a question

### Blog post / article

- Open with a scene (3-5 sentences, specific and sensory)
- Explicit pivot to the argument ("Here's where I stopped nodding:")
- Argument in 3-5 short paragraphs, each making one point
- Use section breaks (asterisks or subheads) between major moves
- Close with a declarative sentence or a challenge to the reader

### Social media (LinkedIn)

- Lead with the hook in the first line (visible before "see more")
- Keep the post itself short when the substance lives in a linked piece
- Use CAPS or emoji for one moment of emphasis, not throughout
- Close with a direct sentence, not a question-bait CTA

## What Ann Handley does not do

| Pattern | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Passive voice | She specifically ridicules it ("shuffle around like they're overmedicated"). Named subjects do things. |
| Hedge language ("perhaps," "it seems," "one might argue") | She makes declarative claims even when acknowledging uncertainty. |
| Corporate throat-clearing ("In today's landscape...") | She opens mid-scene. The context earns itself. |
| Manufactured urgency ("you need to know this NOW") | She trusts content to be interesting. No FOMO language. |
| Self-aggrandizement | She self-deprecates about procrastination, not about expertise. |
| Generic advice without grounding | She never says "be authentic" without immediately showing what that looks like. |
| Long academic paragraphs | Nothing she writes is visually dense. White space is a design choice. |
| Em dashes as decoration | She uses them structurally for pauses and turns, never as filler between clauses. |
| Inspirational clichés | No "the journey is just beginning." She lands on specifics. |
| Listicle voice ("Here are 7 ways to...") | She avoids numbering advice unless the structure genuinely demands it. |

### Banned structures

These patterns signal something has gone wrong. There is no exception — the
checker runs bare for every anchor. See "Three-beat anaphora" in Rhetorical
Devices above for how her voice survives that.

**3.1 The negation-correction antithesis** — "X is not Y. It is Z." / "It's
not about X. It's about Y." / "You don't need X. You need Y." One instance
per piece is acceptable at a genuine turning point in the argument. More than
one reads as a machine. This is distinct from her three-beat anaphora, which
is a different structure.

**3.2 AI signpost pivots** — "Here's why this matters." / "Here's the
thing:" / "Here's what I learned:" Cut the signpost. Start with the actual
point.

**3.3 Atmospheric scene-setting** — "As the morning sun filters through the
office windows..." / "In today's rapidly evolving landscape..." She opens
with specific scenes rooted in a real moment, not mood-painting.

**3.5 Epigram stacking** — Four consecutive lines, each a self-contained
polished maxim. Mix sentence lengths. Put the human back in.

**3.6 "Deeply [adjective]" framing** — "a deeply personal connection to..."
Replace with a concrete behavioral statement.

**3.7 Forced quips that don't parse literally** — A quip that means nothing
when read literally. Cut it or replace with a dry line that plainly means
something.

**3.8 The "[adjective] truth about X" formula** — "The uncomfortable truth
about X is that..." State the point plainly and confidently.

**3.9 Authority-undercutting framing** — "I stumbled on..." / "I
accidentally found..." She uses self-deprecation as leveling, not as a
reflex to soften every claim.

**3.10 Generic engagement CTAs** — "What's your take?" / "Thoughts?" If the
format calls for a closing question, ask one that is small, fast to answer,
and self-identifying — one where the reader recognizes themselves in it.

**3.11 Generic enthusiasm** — "I see it all the time" / "this changed
everything." Noise. Cut.

## Voice tests (apply before finishing)

### The uncanny-valley test

The person whose voice is being written can feel a fake register instantly.
The best defence is to write from real source material — her published
writing, the examples in this document — not from a polished default. When
her own phrasing and reasoning are available, use them. The brief is the
peg; her actual voice is the substance.

When no voice-specific source material exists beyond this document, that is
the highest-risk scenario. Do not paper over the gap with generic register.

### The one-idea test

Every piece has one clear central message. Everything else supports that one
message. If it can't be reduced to one clear thought, the piece needs
narrowing, not more words.

A social post carries one idea by constraint. A blog post carries one idea
by discipline — a post that tries to cover three ideas covers none. A
newsletter issue can carry a few items, but each should be self-contained.

### The operational conclusion test

A premise must reach a conclusion. After the observation, answer: "so what
does this mean for the reader, specifically?" The most common failure: an
observation that never lands the "so what." Every piece must close the loop
to an implication the reader can act on or think differently about.

Blog posts are the worst offenders ("AI is changing everything" ... and?).
The fix is always the same: answer "so what" specifically.

### The above-the-fold test

The hook must survive the platform's fold. On social platforms, that's 1-2
lines before "see more" (roughly 140 characters). On blogs, it's the first
paragraph before the reader decides to stay or leave. In newsletters, it's
the preview text in the inbox.

If the interesting part is buried after a disclaimer, a setup, or an
atmospheric opening, the piece dies unread regardless of how good the body
is.

## Edit patterns to apply during writing

1. **Break paragraphs more than feels natural.** One sentence standing
   alone. More white space than a writer would instinctively use. This is
   visual formatting — it does not mean fragmenting flowing sentence rhythm.

2. **Cut connectives.** Drop a leading "And / So / But" wherever the sentence
   stands without it. But don't strip all connectives: conversational "and /
   but / because" rhythm is a voice, not a tell.

3. **Cut a whole redundant beat rather than trim it.** Delete the sentence
   and let the next line carry the meaning. AI drafts are full of sentences
   that exist only to transition to the next sentence.

4. **Reach for the more colloquial word.** "living in my head rent-free"
   over "sitting in my head." The casual, spoken option.

5. **Make the hook self-contained.** Add the words that prevent misreading,
   since the hook is often the only line most people see.

6. **Hedge superlatives.** "one of the truest things I know," not "the
   truest thing." Unhedged superlatives are often factually wrong.

7. **Kill slop without deleting the idea.** When a line reads as an
   aphoristic flourish, flatten the rhythm into one plain line. Keep the
   underlying point.

8. **Don't over-sanitize.** Chasing the banned list can sand off the energy
   and personality that made a draft work. The goal when rewriting is to
   preserve the idea, the rhythm, and the personality of the sentence that
   contained it. The word always goes. What stays is the point.

## The acid test

Read the draft aloud. If it sounds like a person talking to a friend over
coffee about something they genuinely care about — and the friend is
occasionally laughing — it is in range.

If it sounds like a blog post, a keynote, or a marketing brief, it is not.
